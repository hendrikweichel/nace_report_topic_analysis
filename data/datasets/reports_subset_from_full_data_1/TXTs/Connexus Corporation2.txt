## Executive Summary

This annual report describes the activities carried out, the results obtained, and the major lessons learned during the fiscal year 2022 (FY22), which covers the period from October 1, 2021, to September 30, 2022.

FY22 was marked by the occurrence of major crises (the COVID-19 pandemic and Russia's War in  Ukraine)  which  demonstrated  the  fragility  of  Sub-Saharan  economies  that  are  still  heavily dependent  on  imports.  The  rising  costs  of  main  horticultural  inputs,  particularly  synthetic fertilizers, and the shortages of diesel fuel highlighted the vulnerability of Senegal's production systems. These external shocks are an opportunity to refine the Nafoore Warsaaji intervention strategy  and  to  build  more  antifragile  systems  designed  to  take  advantage  of  shocks.  Through horticultural hubs, Nafoore Warsaaji has opted for an intervention strategy that incorporates the occurrence  of  frequent  major  crises  with  systemic  effects.  Based  on  this  strategy, Nafoore Warsaaji has managed to carry out substantial activities with the following results:

Promotion of optimal and climate-sensitive irrigation systems that reduce fuel dependency. The promotion of climate-sensitive irrigation systems is a major objective of Nafoore Warsaaji 's intervention  strategy.  In  addition  to  the  12  solar  demonstration  platforms  set  up  by  Holland Greentech  (HGT), Nafoore  Warsaaji collaborated  with  IBRIZ  and Crédit  Mutuel  du  Sénégal (CMS) to facilitate the installation of 247 solar pumps, representing financing of about $810,435 (F  CFA  405,217,300)  and  520  hectares  covered,  to  benefit  small  producers  (of  which  80  are women) in the zones of influence (ZOI). The objective through this partnership is to install 750 solar  kits  in  the  ZOI. Nafoore Warsaaji has  adopted  the  same  approach  with  the  NGO  ICCO Cooperation  and  Bonergie  to  install  18  solar  pumps  (representing  an  investment  of  F  CFA 40,513,306) to benefit members of the Potou women's cooperative. The installation of these solar kits benefited at least 152 people, including 132 women.

Implementation  of  group  purchasing  mechanisms  with  producer  organizations  (POs)  to facilitate  access  to  and  reduce  input  costs. Nafoore  Warsaaji promotes  group  purchasing mechanisms across the ZOI, along with systems to monitor the reception and delivery of inputs. In the Dagana region, members of Interprofession des Producteurs d'Oignons du Sénégal (IPOS) Dagana procured 1,220 kg of onion seeds to plant 305 ha of onion valued at $124,280 (F CFA 62,140,000) through the group purchasing mechanism negotiated by the Nafoore Warsaaji team in collaboration with Société Nationale d'Aménagement et d'Exploitation des Terres du Delta du Fleuve (SAED), La Banque Agricole (LBA) and IPOS Dagana. Monitoring by a supervisor and three  facilitators  has  led  to  better  management  of  seed  reception  and  delivery  (backed  by  the delivery slips system for payment) and successful provision of inputs to the 18 beneficiary POs, which include 812 producers, 68 of whom are women. In the Central Zone, Nafoore Warsaaji adopted the same system in collaboration with Agence Nationale de Conseil Agricole et Rural ( ANCAR), Suxali Mbaym i, Union des Institutions de Mutualiste Communautaire d'Epargne de Crédit (U-IMCEC) and two input suppliers (Tropicasem and the Keur Kabir Seck company) to facilitate access to inputs for 900 onion producers cultivating 85 ha of land. In Podor, a group purchasing system was introduced in the GIE Galle Cheikhou Oumar Tall (Tall GIE) producer network to secure inputs for 78 producers, including 38 women to plant 40ha of onion valued at F CFA 14,541,000 ($9,082).

At the six horticultural hubs, Nafoore Warsaaji facilitated the marketing of $7,624,078 in smallholder produce during FY22. In the Staple Vegetables Hub, Nafoore Warsaaji supports the Agence de Régulation des Marchés (ARM) in regulating horticultural markets. The deployment

of SysStock 2.0, that includes the digitization of consignment notes, made it possible to obtain reliable statistics from the main collection centers in the Niayes and Northern zones where the system has been piloted. During the workshop held on June 16, 2022, in Saint Louis, actors SAED, IPOS, Direction de l'Horticulture (DHORT) and ARM expressed the desire to organize a Comité Régional  de  Développement (CRD)  on  electronic  consignment  notes  to  be  issued  from  the collection centers, indicating interest by various actors in the digitization of consignment notes.

Additionally, the integrated financing models built in partnership with hub aggregators, Tampou Ndiania (TNA) in the Center and Tall GIE in the North, enabled the marketing of nearly 100 MT of  onions  from  valley  smallholders  and  1,650  MT  of  onions  (worth  $484,879)  from  1,386 smallholders, including 228 women. These models are a vehicle for securing credit from partner FIs such as CMS, U-IMCEC and LBA.

To boost the commercial production of tomatoes, Nafoore Warsaaji collaborated with the Comité National de Concertation sur la Filière Tomate Industrielle (CNCFTI), the Bureau de Mise à Niveau (BMN) and LBA to establish a pilot drip irrigation modernization program over an area of 12 ha, benefiting eight small and medium-sized enterprise (SME) producers. This modernization is accompanied by access to quality seedlings through five Youth Nurseries in the Northern Zone which received an initial order of 300,000 industrial tomato seedlings for the next campaign.

In  the  Farm  Basket  Hub, Nafoore  Warsaaji provides  technical  assistance  to  various  SME stakeholders (BaySeddo, Proxalys, Afrikamart, Senegal Progresse, Club Thiossane, Aga Bio) to optimize  their  supply  and  delivery  chains  through  digital  tools. During FY22 Q4, Bay Seddo received approval for F CFA 2.5 billion ($5 million) in financing from Ecobank Senegal and a F CFA 845 million ($1.7 million) investment subsidy from the Challenge Fund for Youth Employment (CFYE) 1  to install a 5,000 MT cold room and frozen French fry production line.

## 1. Introduction

Connexus Corporation is pleased to present the FY22 annual report for Feed the Future Senegal Nafoore Warsaaji , referred to locally as Nafoore Warsaaji, meaning the 'Gardens of Abundance' in the local language of Pulaar.

Nafoore Warsaaji 's purpose is to help smallholder horticulturalists, including those already being supported under existing Feed the Future activities, to enhance horticultural production and to integrate smallholder farmers into existing commercial value chains. Many smallholders became involved in horticulture through previous USAID and Feed the Future livelihood, nutrition, water, and resilience activities, and are now experienced enough to focus their production on commercial horticulture, increasing their household revenues. Nafoore Warsaaji is  designed  to  build  value chain linkages and facilitate smallholder engagement in increasingly lucrative business deals with private  sector  partners,  including  input  suppliers,  Micro  Finance  Institutions  (MFIs),  banks, insurance companies, off-takers, and end market buyers.

During  the  Activity  life  cycle, Nafoore  Warsaaji will  pilot  different  business  models  in collaboration with existing USAID programs. These pilots will help smallholders to develop winwin  partnerships  with  leading  market  actors  to  produce  and  trade  a  broad  range  of  nutritious vegetables  and  fruit  on  local,  regional  and  national  markets.  To  strengthen  these  partnerships Nafoore  Warsaaji will  introduce  new  business  models  such  as  improved  contracting,  tailored agricultural finance products, and risk mitigation tools. The Activity will help producers organize themselves into horticulture hubs to facilitate technology transfer and to provide a 'one stop shop' for private sector partners to simultaneously engage with large numbers of producers. These hubs will also allow secondary businesses, such as Last Mile Entrepreneurs (LMEs) and value-added enterprises, to provide services and products to hub actors and capture more value at the local level.

This Annual report is structured in eleven sections. The report begins by sharing the Activity's theory of change and implementation strategy, including its timeline for intervention (Sections 2 and 3). The intervention areas (Section 4) and exit strategy (Section 5) provide an overview of Nafoore Warsaaji 's  scope of intervention and the integration of sustainability concerns into its implementation strategy.

The co-investment strategy described in Section 6 provides an update on the implementation status of Nafoore Warsaaji 's selected Innovation Fund grants.

Section  7,  structured  into  seven  strategic  areas,  provides  a  detailed  picture  of  the  activities undertaken, results achieved, and key lessons learned during FY22. The implementation status of the sub-activities is detailed in the Implementation Tracking Table (Section 7.8). Cross-cutting activities  (Section  7.7)  provide  a  perspective  on  gender  sensitive  operations,  environmental management, and approved communication activities.

Monitoring and evaluation (M&amp;E) activities (Section 8), whose standard tables are included in the annexes,  provide  a  comprehensive  picture  of  the  Activity's  performance  and  environmental indicators. Budget execution status (Section 9), challenges identified during FY22, and corrective actions initiated (Section 10) complete this annual report.

## 2. Theory of Change

During FY22, Nafoore Warsaaji continued testing the following hypothesis:

IF financial and insurance service providers work with buyers, off-takers, input suppliers, lastmile  agents,  and  Farmer  Groups  to  provide  innovative  financial  and  insurance  solutions  that promote  win-win  collaborations,  control  risk,  expand  access  to  technology  solutions,  while minimizing the collateral required to borrow on terms adapted to horticultural production cycles.

IF buyers work with off-takers to replace imported produce with locally produced horticultural produce that is safe and high-quality purchased at competitive and fair prices.

IF off-takers/intermediaries  conduct  win-win  deals  with  growers  to  enable  the  aggregation, storage, value added and timely transport of produce safely to end-market buyers.

IF horticulture growers' access bundled technologies and technical assistance packages from input suppliers,  buyers,  and  LMEs  to  increase  the  quantity,  quality,  and  safety  of  locally  produced horticultural produce.

THEN commercial  investments  in horticultural value chains will increase substantially, benefitting at least 50,000 smallholder farmers (at least 60 percent women) in Feed the Future (FTF) ZOI and in the Niayes Zone in terms of improved incomes, food security, and nutrition for their families. Nafoore Warsaaji will complement Feed the Future Senegal Dooleel Mbay ( Dooleel Mbay )  by improving market linkages and access to finance for horticulture value chain actors, thereby helping to increase household incomes of smallholders. In the Niayes, Nafoore Warsaaji will continue relying on existing farmers organizations, firms, public agencies, and other private actors to build the foundation of the horticulture hubs.

## 3. Implementation Strategy and Phases of Intervention

## 3.1. Nafoore Warsaaji 's Implementation Strategy

Horticulture Hubs : Nafoore Warsaaji's strategy is to structure local horticultural value chains into 'hubs.' A hub is a virtual entity, made up of alliances and deals among sellers, suppliers, and buyers linked by business relationships around a specific crop or set of crops. As it evolves, the hub will diversify in the number and type of actors that interact through it, and will expand to serve local,  regional,  national,  and  even  international  markets. Nafoore  Warsaaji assumes  that  the establishment of the different hubs in specific geographic areas will foster a competitive, inclusive, dynamic, and resilient local agro-economy. The hubs will serve as vehicles for the introduction of new  technologies,  as  incubators  for  Medium,  Small  and  Micro  Enterprises  (MSME),  and  as laboratories to test innovative technologies and business models which can be scaled up by the private sector.

Across Senegal, Nafoore Warsaaji is  providing  sustained  technical  assistance  to  anchor  firms, MSMEs and smallholders at six emerging horticulture hubs. The graphic below gives an overview of the architecture of the six emerging hubs, within which players with heterogeneous profiles collaborate and compete.

## Anti-Fragility: Resilience 2.0

Anti-fragile systems become stronger as they are stressed. Coined  by  Nassim  Taleb,  anti-fragile  systems  develop unique  characteristics  that  allow  them  to  face  uncertainty and  flourish  by  repeated  exposure  to  shock  and  stresses. Building on USAID's Resilience Framework, Connexus has developed its Anti-Fragile Market Systems (AFMS) Framework to strengthen agricultural market systems in five key  dimensions:  Optionality,  Innovation,  Trust,  Diversity and Decision-Making.

advantage of emerging opportunities and mitigate emerging threats.

The AFMS strengthens five primary dimensions to make Nafoore Warsaaji's horticulture hubs more antifragile:

- Optionality: Ensure multiple options for inputs, support services, information, marketing, finance, and risk mitigation at all levels of the hub, accessible to all actors.
- Innovation: Align  risk  and  rewards  in  market  transactions,  improve  entrepreneurial capacity, and promote healthy attitudes toward informed risk-taking and failure.
- Trust: Improve  transparency  and  win-win  dynamics  and  business  attitudes  among partners, balancing competition and collaboration and building cooperation among actors.
- Diversity: Focus on inclusiveness in terms of women and youth as well as a diversity of business models and approaches and ensure that barriers do not hinder a broad variety of business types or entrepreneurs emerging in the hub.
- Decision-Making: Improve how actors collect, analyze, and diffuse data, as well as how they use data to make timely, evidence-based decisions.

## 3.2. Phases of Intervention

Phase 1: Innovate and Pilot (Years 1- 2). In Years 1 and 2, Nafoore Warsaaji focused on building the  foundations  of  the  various  hubs  to  test  different  hub  types  and  innovations  in  technology, business models, risk mitigation measures, and finance. The Activity worked to establish win-win partnerships  among  different  kinds  of  firms,  finance  partners,  and  producer  groups  and  to accompany them during the first two agricultural campaigns to ensure performance.

Phase 2 (Years 3-4): Diversify, Replicate, and Scale. As the anchor partnerships solidify and Private  Sector  Engagements  (PSE)  demonstrate  success  in  piloting  innovations  in  technology, inputs,  finance,  production,  risk  management,  and  logistics,  we  will  focus  on  replicating  and scaling up successful innovations and diversifying the types of actors involved at the hubs. We will also work with FIs to improve finance and risk management and implement digital information platforms to support the scaling of the successes from the first phase of the project.

Phase 3 (Years 3-5): Link Hubs and Ensure Hub Antifragility and Sustainability. As the hubs diversify and scale, we will transition the project focus on linking hubs to each other to facilitate regional trade and technical exchanges, lesson sharing, and collaboration among hub actors. We will continue to link more smallholder farmers, especially subsistence farmers, women, and youth groups, to engage in commercial agriculture and participate with cooperatives and producer groups in  deal  making  and  contract  negotiations  with  hub  actors.  We  will  ensure  that  each  hub  is developing the key attributes of antifragility, leading them to become sustainable ecosystems by the end of the Activity. Finally, we will transition management and oversight fully to local partners to continue to scale the hubs into the future.

## 7. Activity Progress Reporting

## 7.1 Activity Area 1: Collaborating, Learning, Adapting (CLA)

## 7.1.1 Pursue Activity Learning Agenda and Conduct Foundational Activities

As a Small Business Applied Research (SBAR) Activity, Nafoore Warsaaji has made the CLA a central pillar  of  its  implementation strategy. The central question this Activity aims to answer revolves around the business model(s) that should facilitate the insertion of small-scale producers (especially youth and women) into sustainable market systems, and sets the tone for the strategic directions adopted. The implementation of the six horticultural hubs across the ZOIs is also part of this dynamic to confirm or deny this central question and the six research sub-questions. The activities carried out and the results obtained during FY22 have provided some answers to the six established research questions.

## Question 1: What strategies and business models promote the inclusion of small farmers in commercial and competitive horticultural value chains and enhance the availability of healthy, nutritional, and diverse foods for the benefit of women in reproductive age and children under 2 years of age?

The barriers small producers face in participating profitably in commercial horticultural markets include: the small scale of products, high quality standards of buyers, and the absence of options regarding marketing channels at the regional level. In response Nafoore Warsaaji initiated  the process of establishing six horticultural hubs-- regional ecosystems of actors that include input suppliers, service providers, and off-takers---  as  a primary strategy to strengthen the ability of small producers to work together with other value chain actors to produce and sell to various urban.

The  participation  of  more  than  19,100  producers  in  commercial  transactions  during Nafoore Warsaaji 's Phase 1, valued at $15,058,269, suggests that the horticultural hubs approach remains the most appropriate business model to date for the inclusion of small producers in competitive and  sustainable  market  systems.  In  the  "Saloum  Horticole"  and  'Casamance  Naturelle' horticultural  hubs, Nafoore  Warsaaji introduced  the  adoption  of  a  commercial  intermediation mechanism through LFMs, who act as intermediaries between market gardening groups and local

markets. This is one of the major innovations underway in the gradual transformation of market gardening groups into commercial entities. While reinforcing their original role as a source of healthy and nutritious food for rural households, the inclusion of these market garden groups in commercial markets allows them to improve the income of women and households. The seven LFMs achieved  revenues  of  103,101,800  F  CFA  ($206,204)  during  FY22,  attesting  to  a  new commercial momentum present within the market garden groups involved.

For  the  improvement  of  the  availability  of  healthy  and  nutritious  products  for  women  of reproductive age and children under 2 years of age, Nafoore Warsaaji will collect additional data during the next fiscal year to provide more insight.

## Sub-question 1.1: What are the factors contributing to a better inclusion of women and youth in the horticultural VC and how can they be integrated into the business models being deployed?

The baseline study conducted by the Activity highlighted factors hindering the inclusion of women and youth in horticultural value chains. Among these factors, the major constraints were access to land, credit, water and quality inputs. Beyond establishing horticultural hubs as a vehicle for youth and women's inclusion, Nafoore Warsaaji continued improving access to solar-powered irrigation systems  through  partnerships  with  HGT,  IBRIZ,  Bonergie  and  ICCO  Corporation.  These partnerships led to the installation of 277 solar pumping kits across the ZOIs Covering 551 ha. In collaboration with CMS, IBRIZ installed 247 kits, covering an area of 520 ha and generating F CFA 405,217,300 in sales. 35% of the solar irrigation system beneficiaries are women. However, there are variations depending on the zone of intervention, such as in the Potou and Gandiol area where  more  than  90%  of  the  beneficiaries  are  women.  For  access  to  quality  inputs, Nafoore Warsaaji brought  together  input  suppliers  and  local  distributors  during  a  workshop  titled 'suppliers day' which took place from June 26 to 30, 2022. A series of workshops for women's groups on fertilization and crop protection techniques in collaboration with input suppliers Djoloff Agricole, Eléphant Vert and Agropharm was also part of the strategy to improve women's access to quality inputs.

## Sub-question 1.2: What are the challenges to accessing finance in the horticultural value chain and how can they be overcome to expand smallholder and small business access to working and investment capital?

The hub strategy helps lower risk for actors across the board by improving access to finance and inputs, increasing the ability to scale and attract buyers through aggregation, improving quality control  of  produce  at  a  central  level  and  increasing  data  about  markets  for  producers,  finance providers and aggregators. The increase in bank funding for horticulture totaling approximately $3,540,462 in FY22 confirms the relevance of Nafoore Warsaaji 's hub approach to facilitating access to funding. The successful introduction of Collateral Management Agreements in financing horticultural product stocks made it possible to provide an alternative guarantee or collateral to obtain bank credit lines better suited to cover the needs for workflow capital of the horticultural hub stakeholders. The same applies to the horticultural credit-insurance coupling, which is now systematic at LBA and has been extended to U-IMCEC. This credit-insurance coupling mechanism remains one of the powerful mechanisms to increase the interest of FIs in financing horticultural value chains, which are usually perceived as high risk.

## Sub-question 1.3: What value chains and market segments favor smallholders?

In FY22 Nafoore Warsaaji 's focused on the following value chains identified during Phase 1 as key market segments for smallholder and women's integration into commercial horticulture:

- The market segment of African-type vegetables for export mobilizes more women producers because of the low input cost and robustness of these crops, requiring lower technical skills and operational expenses.
- The urban, peri-urban and supermarkets market segments for staple vegetables such as onions and potatoes have a large, stable market demand.
- The  local  and  regional  vegetable/fruit  market  segments  (loumas,  local  boutiques,  regional markets) for smaller quantities of products coming from market gardens in the Center, South and Upper SRV areas. These local markets, which are closer and easier to access, remain the primary destinations for horticultural produce from the 'Saloum Horticole' and 'Casamance Naturelle' hubs.
- Finally, the urban retail market segment (local stores, urban households, restaurants, hotels, boutiques) represents higher value segments. Nafoore Warsaaji , through the development of the Farm Basket (Ndungu) Hub, seeks to reinforce the position of specialized partner SMEs ( Club  Tiossane,  Afrikamart,  Bay  Seddo,  Senegal  Progresse,  AGA  Bio,  Proxalys etc)  and women retailers to services these markets.

Sub-question  1.4:  What  business  models  promote  and  strengthen  the  resilience  of  small producers and small businesses throughout the value chain?

Through its horticultural hub approach, Nafoore Warsaaji seeks to provide small-scale producers with  options  and  opportunities  at  key  leverage  points  (finance,  input  supply,  risk  mitigation, marketing) as well as access to innovative technologies and support services to strengthen their resilience  to  internal  and  external  shocks.  A  mid-term  evaluation  planned  during  Phase  2  will naturally shed more light on the capacity of the horticultural hubs to strengthen the resilience of smallholder  producers  and  SME  stakeholders.  The  gradual  adjustment  of Nafoore  Warsaaji 's implementation strategy to embrace the concepts of 'anti-fragility' is designed to strengthen the resilience of these MSMEs and commercial smallholders and permit them to benefit from shocks and stresses going forward.

## 7.1.6.2. Environmental Mitigation and Monitoring Plan (EMMP) and PERSUAP

During FY22, Nafoore Warsaaji conducted outreach to stakeholders at the various horticultural hubs to ensure compliance with the guidelines set out in the EMMP and Pesticide Evaluation Report and Safer Use Action Plan (PERSUAP). In addition to HGT monitoring these guidelines at the demonstration sites, Nafoore Warsaaji also raised awareness of USAID's requirements via business meetings held with input suppliers and Producer Organizations. In addition, within the framework of the EMMP plan, the M&amp;E team expressed concern about burning of mulch by certain producers. Thus, the M&amp;E team made recommendations to producers to collect organic matter  for  mulching  in  order  to  avoid  the  deterioration  of  the  soil  quality  by  burning.  Other recommendations  relating  to  the  Activity  EMMP  include:  (1)  the  use  of  living  hedges  and windbreaks on garden plots; (2) limiting water loss through regular checks of the irrigation outputs, supply lines and bunds; (3) the use of personal protective equipment (PPE) during phytosanitary treatments; and (4) the non-use of chemicals prohibited in PERSUAP. Observations from the field visits show that the recommendations of the previous missions are generally respected. ## a. Expand business meetings to bring options closer to the remote areas.

## 7.2.3 Private Sector Engagements

PSEs  (formerly  known  as  Public  Private  Partnerships  [PPP])  are  formally  structured  coinvestments where Nafoore Warsaaji is providing innovation grant funds to buy down risk and leverage investments from a private sector partner to test a new market or business model.

Nafoore  Warsaaji 's  approach  to  PSEs  couples  Innovation  Fund  co-investment  grants  with technical assistance. In addition to the fundraising operations necessary to implement the selected projects,  the  technical  assistance  provided  by Nafoore  Warsaaji allows  for  a  trial-and-error mechanism and corrective actions to allow recipients to adapt to the challenges encountered. This trial-and-error mechanism enabled adjustment to be made to the various projects selected based on the evolution of the respective business environments. ## 7.4.2 Organizing and Developing Horticulture Hubs in the ZOI

By definition, a hub is a virtual entity, made up of constellations of regional sellers, suppliers, and buyers linked by business relationships around a specific horticultural crop or set of crops. As soon as several partnerships emerge around a product or a portfolio of horticultural products, a hub gradually begins to take shape as transactions multiply, infrastructure and services specific to those crops  develop,  and  financing  becomes  more  widely  available  to  fund  more  businesses  and transactions. Nafoore Warsaaji is  promoting hubs based on the specificities of the intervention areas, key local value chains, and the profiles of the hub actors to gradually speed up the emergence of horticultural hubs.

In the following paragraphs, we discuss the architecture of the six hubs established in the ZOI and elaborate on activities carried out and the results achieved during FY22 for the different hubs.

## 7.4.2.4 The 'Casamance Naturelle' Hub

Nafoore Warsaaji is  working with Casamance buyers and producers, including CASAGRI and CASADEV, to establish a horticulture hub that is developing a brand of horticultural products based on the Casamance's reputation of being less industrial and more 'natural.' ## 7.4.2.6 The Farm Basket (Ndungu) Hub

Each of the 15 producers received a phytosanitary treatment kit composed of nematicides, fungicides, insecticides, and herbicides. All the products used comply with PERSUAP  regulations. The challenge for the next few years will be to work to accelerate the operational phases of their project.

Nafoore Warsaaji also succeeded in mobilizing the additional investment required by Jabba Sarl from Locafrique totaling F CFA 60 million ($120,000). Work is underway to finalize the 1,500MT cold storage facility located in Notto (Niayes area) .

## 9. Challenges and Solutions

Although Nafoore Warsaaji obtained encouraging results in FY22, major challenges were also highlighted.  Among  these  challenges  were  1)  an  increase  in  the  cost  of  horticultural  inputs including diesel for irrigation due to the increase in the cost of sea freight, one of the effects of the pandemic exacerbated by Russia's War in Ukraine; 2) the lack of reliable information at collection centers leading difficulties for ARM to better regulate flows of products in major urban markets; 3)  the  urgent  need  to  address  the  issue  of  conservation  and  storage  infrastructure  in  order  to regulate the flow of products in the main peri-urban and urban markets; and 4) the need for Nafoore Warsaaji to focus more on activities that mobilize women, and to work on constraints related to access to inputs and financing for women.

Market congestion remains one of the main causes of poor sales noted in past years in the main collection  centers  of  the  Niayes  and  SRV.  The  lack  of  reliable  data  on  stock  levels  in  these collection centers made it very difficult for ARM to regulate product flows from the collection zones to the main markets. The use of non-standardized forms to create consignment notes also prevented the proper execution of the orders issued by the Ministry of Commerce to temporarily freeze marketing. These same concerns led Nafoore Warsaaji to initiate the digitization process for commercial transactions by developing a computerized platform for inventory management, SysStock, whose version 2.0 was successfully deployed at the collection centers in the Niayes and SRV. The digitization of consignment notes and their adoption by stakeholders was also a highlight of FY22. These two initiatives have partly helped ARM to better regulate the current marketing campaign for mass-market vegetables since it has reliable information on the volumes of products available  at  the  main  collection  centers.  The  electronic  consignment  notes  helped  reduce  the incidence of fraud that is often noted with paper versions.

Furthermore, Russia's War in Ukraine continues to increase inputs and basic commodities costs. This has negatively  affected onion yields and those expected from the seed potato production program. Nonetheless, Nafoore Warsaaji continued its efforts to bring together input suppliers and local dealers established in the ZOIs by organizing exchange workshops. In addition, Nafoore Warsaaji also partnered with Eléphant Vert to initiate transitions towards alternative fertilizers by offering producers substitutes for chemical fertilizers, notably organic fertilizers, biofertilizers, and biostimulants. This War has also exacerbated fuel shortages, particularly in the Niayes area, where diesel pumps are still dominant. Nafoore Warsaaji continued to promote solar pumps with its partners IBRIZ , Bonergie and HGT during FY22.

If the effects of the War persist, the above-mentioned activities' impact will remain limited. Thus, the supplement fund received to implement the Activity's Phase 2 will enable Nafoore Warsaaji to  implement  proactive  measures  to  maintain  current  performance  and  support  the  purchasing power producers, who remain the most vulnerable segment of the horticultural hubs established across the ZOIs.

This fiscal year was also marked by activities to support women's initiatives. Nafoore Warsaaji facilitated  access  to  financing  and  solarized  irrigation  systems  for  thousands  of  women. These important results come as the result of a dynamic partnership established with NGO ICCO, UIMCEC, IBRIZ, Bonergie and CMS. Focus made on in the horticultural hubs where women are more present, 'Saloum Horticole', 'Casamance Naturelle' and African Indigenous Vegetables hubs, has enabled many women to integrate themselves into commercial value chains (for more details see the narrative parts of sections 7.2 and 7.3 of this quarterly report).

## Aly Ndiaye, a self-taught man at the heart of horticultural market systems

Aly Ndiaye, Fruit and vegetable retailer

<!-- image -->

" Thanks to Nafoore Warsaaji's work, we now know whom to contact if we need inputs, if we want to install an irrigation system, if we need financing, when we want to buy onions... "

"Our experience in the field combined with Nafoore's technical assistance allows us to improve the horticultural sector."

Aly Ndiaye

Aly Ndiaye - Bay Seddo Born in Baidy Ndiaye, Aly Ndiaye is an entrepreneur with an unconventional background. Starting from nothing, he went from Quranic school to small jobs before launching into the sale of onions in 2012 with a budget of 75 000 F CFA ($136).

At a time when many people were embarking on illegal emigration, he preferred to become a Bana-Bana (local street vendor) with the ambition to succeed in his country and create jobs. In 2014, he managed to acquire his own shop selling horticultural products in the central market of Thies.

Then he continued to climb the ladder in the business and in 2019 became the  procurement  manager  of  Bay  Seddo,  a  start-up  specialized  in  the marketing and processing of agricultural products.

As a recipient of Nafoore Warsaaji's Innovation Fund, Bay Seddo is now a dynamic aggregator whose innovations facilitate market access for smallscale producers through grouping centers, and six sorting and packaging centers.

Aly Ndiaye believes that the support of Nafoore Warsaaji has enabled Bay Seddo  to identify and  network  with various stakeholders in the horticulture value chain, including financial institutions.

"  Thanks  to  Nafoore  Warsaaji's  work,  we  now  know  whom  to contact if we need inputs, if we want to install an irrigation system, if we need financing, when we want to buy onions... "

Aly Ndiaye sees this support as a lever to grow business wherever we are within the horticultural corridor.

"Our  experience  in  the  field  combined  with  Nafoore's  technical assistance allows us to improve the horticultural sector"

With his experience, Aly Ndiaye urges more and more young people to follow his example and wants to show them that despite popular belief, it is possible to get rich in the agricultural sector.

## Diama Tech's incubation center, an innovative solution to improve youth's employability

First group of students of the Mboro incubation center

<!-- image -->

The Centre de Perfectionnement Maraicher (CPM) in Mboro was once designed to strengthen the technical capacities of actors in the horticultural value chain, but it remained inoperative for several decades.

With 23 years of expertise in the manufacturing of agrifood  equipment  and  conscious  of  the  importance  of human capital in its strategy to diversify its portfolio of activities,  Diama  Tech  has  set  up  an  incubation  and training  center  for  horticultural  trades  on  the  same site.  Its  primary  mission  is  to  provide  young  people with  the  means  and  skills  necessary  to  boost  their employability and  hatch the entrepreneurial spirit within them.

Dossouba Ndiaye, Student in Food Processing in Mboro

<!-- image -->

"Our salaries will barely be enough to cover all our basic needs. The return to the land is not an empty slogan because I remain convinced that, like our ancestors, agriculture can feed its people, be a livelihood and feed thousands of souls in Senegal.'

Dossouba Ndiaye, Student in Food Processing in Mboro

Through its co-investment mechanism, Nafoore Warsaaji is supporting Diama Tech in this highly innovative project to improve human capital in  the  horticultural  sector,  which  is  one  of  the  major  axes  of  the Senegalese government's plan for emergence. In addition to a contribution  of  FCFA  82.5  million  ($150,000),  Nafoore  Warsaaji provides ongoing technical assistance to Diama Tech to improve other aspects of their project including the installation of a fruit and vegetable processing  unit  and  a  sorting  and  packaging  center  for  horticultural products.

Operational since March 2022 with a first batch of 26 young people from various areas of Senegal, the objective of this incubation center is to train at least 450 young people and women every three years in various horticultural trades: food  processing, water  engineering, horticultural production, and alternative fertilizer production techniques.

An  innovative  instrument  for  the  inclusion  of  young  people  in horticultural value chains, the Center for Incubation and Development in Horticultural Trades also remains a means to reveal the entrepreneurial ambitions of learners. A member of the first cohort of the center, Miss Dossouba Ndiaye, a young woman of 26 years old, a graduate in transit did not hesitate to seize the opportunity to acquire new skills at Mboro's incubation center.

By specializing in packaging, Dossouba wants to contribute to a better conservation and a longer availability of local products on the market. Her dream is to be self-employed and to not depend on anyone. Ms. Ndiaye invites more young people to work on the land to earn their living.

Onion production: The implementation of innovative systems boosts women's competitiveness.

<!-- image -->

"Today, I am delighted and humbled to be chosen to benefit from this program facilitated  by  Nafoore  Warsaaji  and which  has  allowed  me  to  double  the yield  obtained  on  onion  production during this hot season'.

Fatou KA, Horticultural Promoter Niayes area

With a serene gaze, Fatou Ka surveys her field in the Niayes area and savors her first successful onion harvest. A widow in her forties with children, this horticulturalist from the town of Thiep finally sees a light at the end of the tunnel.

For years, she could only farm on 0.25 Ha of her plot, which is suitable for market gardening. Without a proper irrigation system, she had to rely on a diesel-powered machine to pump water. This rudimentary device often broke down and Fatou Ka was restricted by the high cost of fuel. Despite her hard work and tenacity, it was impossible for her plot to perform well.

This year, her income has doubled from 500,000 FCFA to 1,071,000 FCFA. She attributes this radical increase to the introduction of new production techniques brought to her by Feed the Future Senegal Nafoore Warsaji's pilot program.

Now, she farms on an area of 5000m2 with a solar pumping system and an irrigation kit. She obtained record yields using this technology package, which was provided by Holland Greentech (HGT) through Nafoore Warsaaji's Innovation Fund.

In addition to receiving new technology, the beneficiaries of the activity are trained on good agricultural practices, given access to markets, and coached on entrepreneurship and management.

With this support, Fatou Ka distinguished herself in the pilot phase by meeting her financial commitments and successfully adhering to technical instructions on production.

Today, Fatou Ka is delighted to be part of a program designed to optimize the productivity and competitiveness of small family horticultural farms in Senegal.

Based on the lessons learned from this campaign, the beneficiaries of the HGT pilot program will be able to better prepare their production plans for future campaigns to optimize their yields.
